Does AI change the odds?

The question this registry exists to answer, and what the data can say so far.

The question

Most drugs that enter human trials never reach patients. Across the industry, about 1 in 13 candidates that start Phase 1 are eventually approved, and Phase 2 is the steepest step: fewer than 3 in 10 get through it.1

Developers that use AI say they choose better targets and design better molecules. If that is true, it should show up in the clinic as better odds at each phase, faster progress, or both. Testing the claim needs a denominator: every AI-derived molecule that has been dosed in people, counted to a written definition, with what happened to it, including the programs that quietly stopped. Most published counts do not release their list. This registry does, so the comparison below can be checked and recomputed by anyone.

What the data can say so far

For each step, the bar shows what has happened to the 55 strict-cohort molecules that reached it. The interval on the right shows the share of molecules with a known outcome that moved on, with its 95% confidence interval, against the industry rate for 2011–2020.

Why it is too early to call

  • Small numbers. The intervals are wide because few molecules have an outcome yet. At Phase 2, the rate rests on a handful of molecules; at Phase 3, on none.
  • Most are recent. Most of the strict cohort first entered humans in 2022 or later. Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials take years, so most outcomes are still to come.
  • Early outcomes may not be typical. The first molecules to resolve are the earliest entrants and the fastest decisions. They may not represent the cohort that follows.
  • Stopping is not always failure. A program can stop for money, strategy or a merger. The industry figures count those suspensions the same way,1 so the comparison is like for like on that point. But this cohort is mostly small biotechs, whose stops more often reflect funding.
  • Other counts reach other answers. An analysis of AI-native company pipelines to the end of 2023 found Phase 1 success of 80–90% and Phase 2 success of about 40%.2 It counted a different set of molecules under different rules; see Methodology § 6.

What would settle it

Outcomes, mainly from Phase 2. Several strict-cohort molecules are in Phase 2 or Phase 3 and still running. Each quarterly refresh of the registry records what happens to them, and this page recomputes from the data each time.

How this is computed

  • Only the strict cohort is counted. Borderline entries are not.
  • A molecule reached a phase if its furthest phase is that phase or later. It moved on if its furthest phase is later. Combined Phase 1/2 trials count as Phase 1 until the developer reports the Phase 2 part.
  • A molecule that reached a phase but went no further is stopped if its status is stopped, and still running otherwise (including status unknown).
  • The rate is moved on ÷ (moved on + stopped). The interval is the Wilson score interval.
  • Industry rates are phase-transition success rates for 2011–2020: Phase 1 to 2, 52.0%; Phase 2 to 3, 28.9%; Phase 3 to filing, 57.8%.1
